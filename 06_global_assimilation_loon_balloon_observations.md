# Global Assimilation of Loon Stratospheric Balloon Observations

**Authors:** Lawrence Coy, Mark R. Schoeberl, Steven Pawson, et al.  
**Year:** 2019  
**Venue:** Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres, 124(6), 3005–3019  
**Link:** [JGR Atmospheres](https://doi.org/10.1029/2018JD029673)

This paper demonstrates the value of balloon wind observations for weather forecasting. Three months of Loon stratospheric wind data were assimilated into NASA's GEOS data assimilation system, revealing tropical wind analysis errors of up to 8 m/s and significantly improving forecast quality in data-sparse regions.

---
## Motivation
- There is a significant lack of direct, in-situ wind temperature observations in the stratosphere, especially over oceans and remote areas
- Numerical Weather Prediction models struggle with biases in stratospheric circulation, which negatively effects long term weather forecasting.
## Core Methodology
**Data assimilation of superpressure balloon wind observations into a global NWP system** to quantify the impact of in-situ stratospheric measurements on tropical weather analyses and forecasts.
### Approach
1. **Data Source:** Wind measurements from Google's Loon superpressure balloons operating at ~18–20 km altitude (50–100 hPa) in the tropical lower stratosphere over a 3-month period.
2. **Assimilation System:** NASA's Goddard Earth Observing System (GEOS) global data assimilation system, which combines a global forecast model with diverse observational inputs to produce atmospheric analyses.
3. **Evaluation:** Impact assessed by comparing analyses and forecasts with and without Loon data assimilation, measuring changes in observation-minus-background (O-B) statistics and root mean square deviation (RMSD) of wind forecasts.

---

## Primary Advantage
**A small number of in-situ balloon wind observations produces outsized improvements in tropical weather analyses and forecasts.** The tropical lower stratosphere is so data-sparse that even modest observational additions substantially correct model errors — errors that propagate downward into 2–10 day surface weather forecasts.
